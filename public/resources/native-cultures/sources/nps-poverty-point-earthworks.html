Monumental Earthworks of Poverty Point

The huge earthworks of Poverty Point in Louisiana, built about 3,000 years ago. This reading leaves out the paragraph about the site as a national monument and the last paragraph, which is about visiting.

Monumental Earthworks of Poverty Point is in the Lower Mississippi Valley. It sits on a slightly raised, narrow landform. It covers more than 400 acres. It is a series of earthen mounds and ridges. They overlook the Mississippi River flood plain in what is now northeastern Louisiana. A society of hunter-fisher-gatherers built it and used it for homes and ceremonies. This was between 1700 and 1100 B.C.E. It is a remarkable achievement in earthen construction in North America. Nothing surpassed it for at least 2,000 years. At its peak 3,000 years ago, it was part of an enormous trading network. The network stretched for hundreds of miles across the continent.

flood plainnoun

flat land next to a river that is covered by water when the river floods

An apparently large, sophisticated society built this impressive complex. It was an engineering feat. It was made of five mounds, six concentric semi-elliptical ridges, and a central plaza. Shallow depressions separated the ridges. Researchers and archeologists have not been able to work out why Poverty Point was built and designed this way. But they estimate that around 53 million cubic feet of soil were moved to create the ridges and mounds. Archeologists continue to work to learn about the society and the reason for the mound complex. Some of them work at the on-site archeological laboratory at Poverty Point. The population changed by season. But the area was a large, complex settlement. It had year-round residents for over 600 years.

Relatively little is known about the Poverty Point society’s name, language, or religion. Clay cooking balls, spear points, and fishing tools were found at the site. They give us some idea of how the mound builders ate and lived. The jewelry and other objects found at Poverty Point were of high quality, in great variety, and in large numbers. Because of this, some people guess that it may have been a capital for an entire ancient culture. But the size of the complex and the number of objects are not what make Poverty Point remarkable. Research on the society continues. It is clear that the people who moved the earth, basket by basket, were hunter-gatherers. It is unusual that such a society could build the complex system of mounds at Poverty Point.

hunter-gatherernoun

a person who gets food by hunting animals and gathering wild plants instead of farming

Poverty Point sits at the crossroads between the Mississippi River floodplains and Macon Ridge. The people there relied on fish, turtles, frogs, waterfowl, and water plants. Deer, small mammals, nuts, fruits, and seeds were also easy to find. The surrounding area also provided natural resources for shelter, cooking, tool making, and warmth. There was only one natural resource that the people of Poverty Point relied on but could not find in large amounts. It was stone. So they created a transportation system. It brought several tons of rocks and minerals into the area from as far as 1,000 miles away.
